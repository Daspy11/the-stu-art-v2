# SKY SAGA — World Bible (draft 2, after three hostile reads)

## 0. Pitch

Two hundred and thirty years ago the ground gave way and there was nothing under it.
What stayed up is whatever people **kept coming back to**. Nobody knows why. Everyone depends on it.
You are an apprentice sexton in a town of forty-one doorsteps, and your job is to keep it in the sky by walking the same round every morning.

Three materials: **slate, beeswax, rope.** No brass, no goggles, no gears, no elves, no chosen ones.
One verb: **to mind** (as in "mind the step").

---

## 1. What happened

- **The Floor** — the old world. "Back on the Floor." "Floor-made." Place names keep words like *ford*, *bridge*, *port*; people know the definitions and can't picture them.
- **The Give** — cracks opened everywhere and widened over a season. Then the ground *gave*. It didn't fall into anything. Behind it was pale sky with no bottom.
- **The Pour** — the seas ran off the edges of what was left. Eleven years. Legend now.
- **The Long Way** — what is below. It is not dark. It is *bright*: a white-gold haze light seems to come up through. Cloud a mile below, cloud a mile below that, then only pale. Nothing dropped has ever been heard to land.
- Gravity goes one way. The sun crosses overhead as it always did. At noon the very top of the sky has a faint pale-gold cast to it, the same colour as below. Everyone has noticed. It isn't discussed.

### The taboo
"Down" is a swear word. So is "low". Polite people say **"away"** ("her husband went away"). Children say "what the *down*" for the thrill. No window faces below; floors are double-planked so there are no gaps. Looking over the edge is **looking low**. You don't throw things *away* either — which is why every scrap, including night soil, is kept and used.

---

## 2. Stay (the magic)

Some matter doesn't fall. The property is called **stay**. It is treated like magnetism: measurable, tradeable, taxed, not understood.

### The rhyme (what everybody knows)

> Old holds.
> Used holds.
> Left lets go.
> Parted pulls —
> *and don't look low.*

1. **Old holds.** Stay builds over years, never quickly.
2. **Used holds.** Things that are handled, walked on, come back to hold better than things left alone. A doorstep beats a boulder. It has to be the *same* comers over a long time: a crowd of strangers barely counts, and past a point more traffic adds nothing.
3. **Left lets go.** Take a kept thing from its people and its stay drains over months and years. (You can carry your step to a new house. You can't sell it to a stranger and have it hold.)
4. **Parted pulls.** Split a kept thing and the pieces pull toward each other, the short way home, at any distance anyone has tested. Both ways: the bell pulls to the clapper exactly as hard as the clapper pulls to the bell.

Only things that **keep their place** take stay. Water never has; nobody expects air to, which makes the air an open embarrassment (§3).

Stay is counted in **years** ("a sixty-year stone") and read with a **plumb**: a bob of spent slate on a waxed thread, which goes slack and drifts near strong stay.

### What the trades know

- **Kept things** — hearthstones, doorsteps, pews, wells, bells, cradles, milestones, grave-markers. *Floor-things* are the richest and are a finite inheritance.
- **Wax takes stay; iron doesn't.** So does loft-timber and the wool of a flock that has walked one terrace for generations. Bees and pigeons are the two animals everyone keeps. Nobody has a good reason why they should matter.
- **Minding** — keeping stay topped up by going round and *using* things: standing on the step, striking the bell, drawing the cup. Dull, daily, and the most important job there is. The officer responsible is the **sexton**, who is therefore both gravedigger and chief engineer. Children of the away are given to the sexton as apprentices — "they'll be visiting anyway."
- **The dead hold.** Towns bury in the **keel**, the built underside. A visited grave is among the steadiest things known.
- **Homers** — a flier knocks a chip off their own doorstep and carries it on a thread. It points home. It is the only compass. *A homer slows a fall*, everyone says. It does. Never enough.
- **Tells** — the doorstep stays at the door. From its underside a waxed line runs down through the deck into the keel, with a small slate weight on the end: the **tell**. Because the step pulls toward its chip, the tell hangs slightly off plumb, toward wherever the flier is. The keel-walk is where the sexton reads the tells each morning.
- **Hanging true** — a tell that stops leaning and hangs dead straight, line taut. The chip is below. That is how you learn someone fell.
- **Cutting the thread** — a step that hangs true is pulling *toward the pale*, and it drags its town with it. So a year and a day after a tell goes true, the family cuts the thread: the chipped corner is struck off the step and rendered, the pairing ends, the name moves in the Ledger from *away* to *gone*. It is the law everywhere, it is the funeral, and it is always done. Which is why nobody alive has seen what a pair does after it has hung true for longer.
- **Kedging** — how towns travel. The clapper of the town bell is flown ahead by a **lead-runner** and pegged to a **kedge-rock** — a heavy wild island that won't be dragged. The bell pulls toward its clapper and the town follows at walking pace. Routes are chains of kedge-rocks; charts are lists of them; wars are over them. A town without a clapper is **dumb** and goes where the wind takes it.
- **Rendering** — the industrial breakthrough, a century old. Ring a kept thing hard enough in a wax bath and its stay goes into the wax. **Cake** holds but never pulls (rendering breaks all pairing), stacks, ships, and can be sold to strangers. It is lossy — a third, at best — and the source is left **spent**.
  - Spent things are wrong in a way people don't discuss. You walk into your rendered house and it is like a word you've said too many times. You know it's your door. It isn't your door.
  - Burning cake gives heat and destroys the lift. Rich cities do it in winter. Poor people regard this as roughly like burning a church.

### What the scholars argue

- The **College of Weights** holds that *stay is age*, a slow mineral ripening, and that rule 3 is about distance from where a thing ripened.
- The heresy is that *stay is use.*
- Neither explains: the standing stone on Hobb's Rock, unvisited by anyone, which held for two centuries and then dropped out of the sky the same spring the swifts didn't come back. Nor why wild islands with seabird colonies sink slower than bare ones. Nor why the best-holding object in any town is always the toll-box.

### Designer truths (never stated in game)

1. Stay is **returning**. Not love — *habit*. Matter resists the fall in proportion to how reliably the same living things come back to it. The Floor was never simply "solid": it was kept, by something that kept coming back, and one season it stopped. What that was is the long mystery of the saga.
2. **The Long Way is a loop.** Far enough down is up; the pale at the zenith is the same pale. Unpaired matter takes centuries to come round — the Floor itself is still on its way, and the first of it is due within a lifetime. A *paired* thing is different. For the first half of its fall the short way home is back up, so the pair slows it and its step drags downward. Past halfway, the short way home is *onward*: the pull now hurries it, and its step, which has hung true, swings to pull **straight up**. Because threads are always cut after a year, no pair has ever been left to reach halfway. Until Latchford's sexton told a lie.

## 3. How anyone is alive

- **Air.** Nobody knows why it didn't go. It is the first question in every natural-philosophy lecture and it is not on the exam. Old people insist it's thinner than it was.
- **Water.** No rivers, no sea. Every slate roof and terrace is a rain-catch; **fog-nets** of spider silk on spars comb cloud into cisterns between rains. Towns follow weather like herds follow grass. Water is rationed by the cup, washing is ash and sand, and a wet week is a holiday.
- **Salt.** A few islands are old sea-bed. Mining them lightens the town above and weakens the rock under it, so salt-towns are rich, short-lived, and owned by the cities. Salt is the second currency.
- **Height is climate, and climate is class.**
  - **The Bright** (high): cold, thin, dry. Grows nothing. Stores never rot, lungs stay clean, and it is as far from the pale as you can get. The great cities sit there because they can afford to.
  - **The Lanes** (middle): where weather comes through and most towns drift and trade. Lanes towns make rope, silk, slate-work and post; they buy their bread from below.
  - **The Damps** (low): warm, wet, fogbound. Everything grows, everything rots, damp-lung by forty. They grow the food and the timber and keep the hives — wax is the Damps' great export and the cities' real dependency.
  - Below the Damps is **Shadow-up**, the height at which the pale is bright enough that your shadow falls on the ceiling. A town seen below Shadow-up is struck from the registers.
  - So: water, food and wax go **up**; cake, salt, law and assessors come **down**.
- **Food.** Terraces, net-bags of soil hung down the flanks (soil is wealth: "dirt-rich"), keel mushrooms, goats because they barely drink, bees, pigeons. The pigeons forage wild rocks and Damps stubble and bring it home as guano, the only fertiliser. Lichen-flour bread in bad years.
- **Iron** is all Floor salvage. Nails are counted and inherited. Smiths are menders. This, not taste, is why everything is slate, rope and felt.
- **Warmth.** No ground, no shelter. Houses are low, round-shouldered, tarred and slate-skirted. Winter is rationed dung.
- **Rope.** Each autumn ballooning spiders rise out of the Damps in their millions — **the Silking**. Towns net it. All rope, thread and fog-net comes from it. The riggers' guild is **the Ropewalk**.
- **Wild islands sink.** A few feet a year. Nobody discusses the long run.

## 4. How people are organised

- **A holding** — a household and its kept things. When you move, you carry the step. Marriage is "sharing a step".
- **A town** (a few hundred) — one island with a built keel. Officers:
  - the **Sexton** minds the stay and the dead;
  - the **Tally** keeps **the Ledger**, the public account of the town's stay, step by step. *In the black*: holding height. *In the red*: sinking;
  - the **Loft** keeps the pigeons, which home to the loft itself wherever it drifts, and are the post and therefore the news.
- **A drift** — towns that follow the same weather and marry each other's children. Towns can **raft up** (lash together), but a raft is assessed as one ledger and one red town sinks the lot, so mostly they don't.
- **The climber cities** — a handful of stacked cities in the Bright, tens of thousands each, held up by cake. They burn stay faster than they can make it. So **assay vessels** work down the Lanes inspecting towns. Assessors bring their own plumbs and take their own height against Shadow-up; the Ledger is a legal paper, not evidence, and a false one is a hanging matter. A town found in the red is declared *sinking* and **relieved** of its kept stones "for the safety of its people", who are resettled in the city keel. Assay vessels fly on cake. Towns kedge at walking pace. Nobody outruns one.
  - Anything *unusual* — a stone holding beyond its years — is listed first and taken first.
  - City keels are vast dovecotes and goat-walks, plus **wearers**: people paid by the hour to do what animals won't — open the same door, sit the same bench. It works a little. The wearers keep changing, and it has to be the same comers.
  - Damps farm-towns are not relieved. They are kept as **tied holdings**, propped with a cake allowance in exchange for grain and wax.
  - The cities are not villains to themselves. Their arithmetic is correct: small towns do sink, and the stones would be lost.
- **The Lofts** — pigeon-keepers answer to each other before any town. The only institution that spans the sky.

---

## 5. Tone

Play and gravity in the same breath, never winking. People are funny because they are practical about something unbearable. One piece of whimsy per scene, and always next to something hard: a measured cup, a cough, a house nobody will go into. Nobody makes a speech.

The horror is quiet and it is *down*. It is bright, patient and has no monsters in it.

## 6. Chapter One — Latchford

Forty-one doorsteps. Low in the Lanes and getting lower. Its bell has been dumb for two years.

- **Mote** (player, 13). Apprentice sexton. Wax-yellow flier's coat four sizes too big; it was their mother's spare. Wants, mostly, to get past the third tell each morning without it being a conversation.
- **Sexton Abel Thrum.** Enormous, slow, bottle-green felt. Has never raised his voice. Two years ago he should have cut Marrin's thread. Instead he has kept her in the Ledger as *away* — so Mote keeps the house and the step — and has been quietly making up the difference out of his own rounds. It is the only dishonest thing he has ever done and it is slowly sinking the town.
- **Deputy.** A pigeon. Deputy Postmaster. Rides on Thrum's hat. Sour, punctual, unimpressed.
- **Mrs. Tunney, the Tally.** Tall, narrow, ink-blue. Counts everything, including your footsteps. She is frightened and deals with it by being exact. She is also right.
- **Brick.** Eleven. Red scarf. Named, unkindly, for a thing with no stay. Is building a skiff out of his family's bathtub, which after four generations of Saturday nights hovers a hand's breadth off the deck. Says "down" as often as possible.
- **Pilot Marrin.** Mote's mother. Latchford's lead-runner. Flew out with the clapper two years ago in clear weather. Her tell has hung true since. Never appears. Her doorstep is Mote's doorstep, and it has a chip out of one corner.
